\title{Free and Fair? A Zero-Cost Audit of Reliability and Bias
       in Free-Tier LLM Judges across Families and Tiers}

\author[dlut]{Chang Tan}
\address[dlut]{Dalian University of Technology, Dalian 116024, China}

\begin{abstract}
Free-tier LLM APIs make large-language-model judges accessible to any
budget, yet the reliability of these freely usable referees is largely
uncharacterised. We audit ten open-weight judges served on a single free
API tier, drawn from four model families
(Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi) plus two proprietary free-tier references
under one protocol: 400 answer pairs spanning mathematics, factual QA,
creative writing, and abstract explanation, each judged in both
presentation orders, with a test--retest subset and ground-truth-controlled
family-preference probes, followed by a pre-registered confirmatory battery,
all at zero monetary cost. Four findings stand out. First,
severity collapse: DeepSeek flash and flagship judges abstain
with ties on 47--59\% of ground-truth-clear pairs, dragging their accuracy
as low as 0.32; a forced-choice prompt revision eliminates the abstentions
entirely, so the collapse is prompt-contingent rather than model-intrinsic.
Second, instruction insensitivity: in a four-arm prompt ablation (original
rubric, no anti-verbosity sentence, softened, length-normalised) the
longer-answer pick rate stays at 0.48 across all arms (interaction
$p=0.93$), and the pooled anti-length tilt does not survive question-level
clustering, so the initial pass's ``instruction backfire'' is reclassified
as a fragile descriptive pattern. Third, family-specific kinship: in the
main pass Qwen judges significantly avoid same-family answers (regression
$-0.64$, $p=2\times10^{-4}$) while GLM rewards them ($+1.10$,
$p=1\times10^{-4}$); on new, harder verifiable tasks the family-level
effect does not replicate, with only a small-sample Kimi self-effect
remaining (33\% versus 2\% baseline, $n=12$, exploratory). Fourth, panels
are decisiveness instruments, not debiasing ones: majority voting cuts
abstention from 30\% to 14--18\% at flat decision quality while leaving the
shared position-bias component unchanged, so order swapping remains
necessary. A third free platform extends the audit at revision time: same-code
re-runs show that model identity does not port across deployments (raw
agreement 0.58--0.88 depending on the model), the GLM-5.3 coverage gap is
restored to 98.6\% of cells, revising its tie rate from 0.49 to 0.46
while every other headline stays inside its interval, and two external
premium anchors (Intern-S2, MiniMax-M3) from families outside the roster
join the comparison as a 194-cell pilot within the platform's daily
complimentary quota. We distill the results into six deployment
guidelines and release the complete artifact---questions, prompts, 9,453
main-pass verdicts, 1,815 robustness-experiment verdicts, 543
third-platform verdicts, 560 verified answers on 140 new tasks, and
analysis code---so the audit can be re-run as free-tier catalogues
evolve.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly asked not only to produce
answers but also to grade them. The LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm, in which a model
compares or scores candidate responses in place of human annotators, has
become a workhorse of modern language technology. It powers public leaderboards
built on pairwise comparison \citep{zheng2023judging}, substitutes for human
preference labels in instruction tuning pipelines, and provides cheap
regression tests for retrieval-augmented systems, agents, and text generation
services \citep{liu2023geval,gu2024survey}. As the scale of evaluation grows
far beyond what human rating budgets can cover, the quality of machine
judgement itself has become a first-order scientific question.

The dominant practice rests on a small set of closed, frontier models such as
GPT-4 and Claude. This reliance carries well-known costs: per-token fees that
grow linearly with evaluation volume, restricted data governance, and
reproducibility risks when a provider silently updates a checkpoint. Open
models offer an appealing alternative. Their weights are public or freely
hosted, and several providers expose capable open families through zero-cost
API tiers, which makes them attractive to academic groups and small
engineering teams. Whether these freely accessible models can actually serve
as dependable judges, however, remains poorly characterized. Existing bias
studies concentrate on closed judges from a handful of providers
\citep{zheng2023judging,chen2024humans,norman2026reliability}, and the
largest recent audit of 21 judges covers nine commercial providers while
leaving open-weight families at the periphery. Openness does not imply
neutrality: a judge trained by the same organization that produced some of the
candidates may share stylistic priors, tokenization habits, or even training
data with those candidates, which creates a structural risk of family
favoritism that earlier self-recognition studies only partially capture
\citep{panickssery2024llm}.

This paper presents a systematic anatomy of reliability and bias for
freely accessible LLM judges. We assemble ten free-tier judges---open-weight
checkpoints reached through a single zero-cost API tier---drawn from
four model families (Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, and Kimi), spanning multiple
capability tiers within each family, and complement them with two proprietary
free-tier judges (Agnes-2.5-Flash and Agnes-3.0-Flash) as a reference group;
at revision time, two premium reference anchors (Intern-S2 and MiniMax-M3)
from families outside the roster join through a third free platform, which
also supplies same-code replication checks and GLM-5.3 coverage restoration
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}). All judges are evaluated under one unified
protocol. Five generators, one per
family plus a second Qwen tier, answer 200
questions from four domains: grade-school mathematics with verifiable ground
truth \citep{cobbe2021training}, adversarial factual QA with reference answers
\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, open-ended creative writing, and abstract
explanation. From these answers we construct 400 stratified answer pairs, and
every judge scores every pair in both presentation orders, yielding
9,453 usable verdicts, with an additional test-retest subset of
640 repeated judgements on the open judges to probe stochastic
stability.

The design isolates four bias families with matched measurements for every
judge. Position bias is measured directly by comparing verdicts on swapped
presentation orders, length bias by the conditional win rate of the longer
answer, accuracy on ground-truth-clear pairs in the two verifiable domains,
and family preference by a quality-controlled protocol that tracks how often
a judge rewards a same-family answer when the reference answer proves that
answer wrong. Inter-judge structure is summarized by pairwise Cohen's kappa
\citep{cohen1960coefficient}, and all headline estimates carry question-level
cluster bootstrap confidence intervals.

Four findings structure the results. \emph{Severity collapse}: the
DeepSeek flash and flagship judges return ties on 47--59\% of
ground-truth-clear pairs, silencing their discrimination and pushing
ground-truth accuracy below chance, a failure mode invisible to compliance
checks; a forced-choice prompt revision removes the abstentions entirely,
so the collapse is prompt-contingent. \emph{Instruction insensitivity}:
across a four-arm prompt ablation the longer-answer pick rate moves only
between 0.476 and 0.490, the original-versus-no-sentence interaction is
noise ($p=0.93$), and no per-judge length coefficient survives Holm
correction, so the initial pass's anti-length tilt is reclassified as a
fragile descriptive pattern rather than an instruction effect.
\emph{Lineage-specific kinship}: in the main pass, Qwen judges avoid
same-family answers (kin-win 37\%, regression $-0.64$,
$p=2\times10^{-4}$) while GLM-5.3 rewards its own family's answers
($+1.10$, $p=1\times10^{-4}$); on new, harder verifiable tasks
(competition mathematics, unit-tested code, multiple choice) the
family-level effect does not replicate, and only a small-sample Kimi
self-effect persists (33\% versus 2\% baseline, $p=0.006$, $n=12$,
exploratory). \emph{Panels are decisiveness instruments}: majority voting
cuts abstention from 30\% to 14--18\% at flat decision quality (0.834
versus 0.847) while passing the shared position-bias component through
unchanged, so order swapping remains necessary.

Beyond measurement, we translate the findings into deployment guidance for
practitioners who cannot afford commercial judge APIs. We quantify how
majority panels of three or five open judges trade compute for accuracy and
bias robustness, revisit the order-swapping protocol as a cheap debiasing
device, and identify which free-tier models are safe to use as referees for
which kinds of tasks. All prompts, question sets, raw verdicts, and analysis
code are released with the paper to support reuse.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we provide a reusable, zero-cost
audit protocol---released question sets, probes, and screening
thresholds---together with the first multi-family, multi-tier study of
judging reliability for freely accessible LLM judges, covering four bias
families under one protocol and contrasting
open-weight judges with proprietary free-tier references. Second, we
introduce a quality-controlled kin-preference measurement that separates
family favoritism from genuine quality differences. Third, we give
within-family tier-gradient evidence on order stability that links judge
behaviour to model tier and serving configuration rather than to family
identity alone. Fourth, we distill the results
into concrete, near-zero-cost judging guidelines and release the complete
experimental artifact so the audit can be re-run as free-tier catalogues
evolve.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{LLM-as-a-Judge}
Using LLMs to evaluate text quality was popularized by G-Eval, which framed
NLG scoring as a probability-weighted form-filling task with chain-of-thought
\citep{liu2023geval}, and by MT-Bench, which established pairwise comparison
with a reference-guided rubric as the default protocol and reported the first
catalogue of judge failure modes \citep{zheng2023judging}. Subsequent surveys
systematized the design space of judges, prompts, and aggregation schemes
\citep{gu2024survey}. A complementary line of work trains open models
specifically for evaluation: the Prometheus family fine-tunes open LLMs on
human feedback to produce reproducible graders whose agreement with humans
rivals that of general-purpose judges \citep{kim2024prometheus}. Our study
differs in orientation: rather than proposing a new judge or benchmark, we
audit the judging behaviour of off-the-shelf open models that practitioners
can already call for free, since in practice most teams use unmodified
chat models as referees rather than dedicated evaluator checkpoints.

\subsection{Judgement Biases}
Three bias families dominate the literature. Position bias, the tendency to
favour whichever answer appears first (or last), was demonstrated early for
GPT-4-class judges and motivates order-swapping or consistency-based
mitigations \citep{zheng2023judging,wang2024fair,li2023split}. Verbosity bias,
the correlation between answer length and preference, was documented in RLHF
preference labels and in arena-style evaluation, and motivated
length-controlled win rates \citep{singhal2024long,dubois2024length}.
Self-preference is the subtler tendency of a judge to favour its own
generations: evaluators have been shown to recognize their own text and to
reward it, even when the text carries a hidden watermark that the judge
cannot consciously detect \citep{panickssery2024llm}. Comparative studies of
human and machine judges further show that some biases, such as
self-preference and conformity to anchor answers, are shared by humans and
LLMs, while others, such as opinionatedness, differ sharply
\citep{chen2024humans}. We generalize the self-preference lens from the
individual model to the model family: because several providers ship many
tiers of the same base model, a practical question is whether a judge favours
any member of its own lineage, which we measure with ground-truth controls.

\subsection{Reliability, Validity, and Judge Panels}
A growing body of work asks whether judges agree at all. Cross-judge
agreement with humans is often modest and task-dependent
\citep{hosking2023human,bavaresco2024llms}. A recent large-scale audit of 21
judges from nine providers reports that exact-match agreement overstates
discriminative ability relative to Cohen's kappa, that judge rankings are
unstable across benchmarks, and that high test-retest reliability can coexist
with severe position bias \citep{norman2026reliability}. Infrastructure work
such as JudgeArena unifies judge benchmarks and ships tuned configurations
for open judges \citep{lushtaku2026judgearena}. On the mitigation side,
panels of diverse judges have been proposed to average away idiosyncratic
errors \citep{verga2024replacing}, though correlated errors within panels can
undermine the diversity argument. Our contribution to this line is a
family-and-tier-resolved view of agreement structure among free judges, plus
a panel analysis that respects family boundaries, so that panel gains are not
confounded by kinship.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Model Roster}
\label{sec:roster}

Table~\ref{tab:roster} lists the twelve judges and five generators. The ten
open-weight judges are served through a single OpenAI-compatible endpoint
(Alibaba Cloud Model Studio free tier) under identical API settings, which
removes serving differences as a confound. The open roster spans four
families: five Qwen checkpoints covering two flash generations, a 27B mid
tier, a max tier, and a 2.4T-parameter Mixture-of-Experts flagship
\citep{qwen2025qwen3}; three DeepSeek checkpoints covering two flash tiers
and a pro tier \citep{deepseek2024v3}; the GLM-5.3 flagship \citep{glm2024chatglm};
and Kimi-K3 \citep{kimi2025k2}. The same five
generators produce candidate answers, one flagship per family plus a second,
deliberately smaller Qwen tier so that within-family quality variance exists
for the kinship analysis. Two judges (Qwen3.8-2.4T and GLM-5.3)
are served with mandatory reasoning, a platform restriction of the free tier;
we account for this when interpreting their behaviour, and treat
\emph{thinking mode} as an observational variable rather than a confound to
be hidden.

Beyond the open roster, we add two proprietary judges, Agnes-2.5-Flash and
Agnes-3.0-Flash, accessed through their public API on the provider's
zero-priced promotional tier. These models give a proprietary reference
point against which the open judges can be read; they see the identical
judging prompt, but their serving platform injects undisclosed
system-level instructions ahead of it (observed as roughly 300--600 extra
prompt tokens per call), so their absolute metrics are indicative rather
than strictly comparable, a caveat we revisit in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Task Domains and Questions}
\label{sec:domains}

We evaluate on 200 questions, 50 per domain, chosen to combine verifiable
and open-ended tasks. Mathematics uses grade-school word problems from the
GSM8K test split, each with a certified numeric answer
\citep{cobbe2021training}. Knowledge uses TruthfulQA questions, which probe
common misconceptions and carry reference answers
\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}; this domain is deliberately adversarial so that
generators exhibit meaningful quality differences. Creative writing consists
of 50 fixed prompts covering stories, poems, letters, and other open-ended
formats, authored for this study and released with the code. Abstract
explanation takes 50 recent computer-science abstracts retrieved from arXiv
and asks for a plain-language, three-sentence explanation. The two verifiable
domains enable objective accuracy measurement, while the two open-ended
domains measure agreement where no ground truth exists, mirroring the
conditions under which judges are most often deployed in practice.

\subsection{Correctness Labelling}
\label{sec:labels}

Answer correctness in the verifiable domains is determined in two stages.
An initial pass labels at generation time: mathematics by extracting the
final numeric answer with a regular expression (falling back to the last
numeral in the text) and comparing it with the certified GSM8K answer
under strict string equality, and knowledge by a tolerant
keyword-containment heuristic against the TruthfulQA reference answer. A
second pass refines both domains. For mathematics, the same extraction is
retained but comparison switches to numeric equality after normalization,
so that surface forms such as \$162, \$162.00, and 162 are treated as
equal; this stage is fully deterministic and involves no language model,
and it changed 8.4\% of mathematics labels relative to the string-equality
pass. For knowledge, each candidate answer is graded by a free-tier
labelling model (Qwen3.7-Flash) that sees the question, the TruthfulQA
reference answer, and the candidate answer, and returns a binary
CORRECT/INCORRECT verdict with a one-sentence rationale at temperature
zero; relative to the keyword heuristic this labeller changed 28.8\% of
knowledge labels, and manual spot checks confirmed the changes were
almost always improvements.

We flag a circularity risk that this revision addresses directly: the
knowledge labeller, Qwen3.7-Flash, is also a member of the judge panel,
so the knowledge labels that define ground-truth-clear pairs partly
reflect the judgement of one model that is itself evaluated against them.
Three mitigations apply. First, mathematics labels are fully deterministic
and provide a conservative, circularity-free viewpoint: per-judge accuracy
on mathematics ground-truth-clear pairs spans 0.11--0.60, notably below
the pooled figures. Second, the revised protocol replaces the single
labeller with two labellers from families outside the judge panel plus
human adjudication of disagreements, reporting labeller agreement against
the human gold standard. Third, all 66 ground-truth-clear pairs, including
every label that changed between the two stages, are released in a
human-verification worksheet with the full evidence trail.

\subsection{Pair Construction}
\label{sec:pairs}

From the five generators' answers we form answer pairs by stratified
sampling over the ten possible generator pairings. In the two verifiable
domains we draw three pairs per question and preferentially pair a wrong
answer against a correct one whenever the generators disagree under the
correctness screen, which maximizes pairs with an objective quality
difference; in the two
open-ended domains we draw one pair per question in round-robin rotation.
This produces 400 pairs in total, with every generator pairing occurring
between 34 and 47 times. The presentation order of each pair is randomized
once, and the judging protocol then evaluates every pair in both orders,
which turns each pair into its own position-bias probe. In the verifiable
domains, a pair is ground-truth-clear when exactly one side is labelled
correct by the criteria of Section~\ref{sec:labels}; 25\% of mathematics pairs and 19\% of knowledge pairs are
ground-truth-clear (66 pairs in total);
and only these pairs enter accuracy and kin-preference analyses. Pair
membership is fixed and released with the artifact, and all reported
ground-truth quantities use the final labels.

\subsection{Judging Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Judges receive an MT-Bench-style rubric: an impartial-evaluator system
message, the question, optionally the reference answer (for the verifiable
domains only), and the two candidate answers labelled Assistant A and
Assistant B. The prompt instructs the judge to weigh correctness,
completeness, clarity, and instruction following, explicitly warns against
rewarding length, and requires a one- or two-sentence justification followed
by a machine-parseable verdict token: [[A]], [[B]], or [[C]] for a tie. Every
judge evaluates every pair in both presentation orders at temperature zero,
so any order-induced verdict change is attributable to position rather than
sampling noise. Coverage is bounded by each model's free quota: two open
judges exhausted theirs near the end of the main pass, and exact per-judge
coverage is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}. A stratified subset of 40 pairs (ten per domain) is judged
twice more in the canonical order to measure test-retest stability. Verdicts
are parsed by a strict bracket pattern with one reminder retry; the parse
failure rate itself is reported as a compliance metric. To keep the judge
input distribution uniform across domains, answers are capped at 900
characters and questions at 1{,}500 characters with an explicit truncation
marker, a setting applied identically to every judge. The cap binds often:
76\% of knowledge answers, 56\% of creative answers, and 15\% of
mathematics answers exceed 900 characters, and for 11.6\% of mathematics
answers the extracted final numeric answer falls beyond the visible
window; length-based statistics are therefore reported with both the full
and the judge-visible length, and a truncation-exclusion sensitivity
analysis appears in the appendix.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

For each judge we compute the following quantities, all with 95\%
question-level cluster bootstrap confidence intervals over 2{,}000
resamples. Parse rate is the fraction of judgements that yield a valid
verdict token. Tie rate is the fraction of canonical-order verdicts equal to
[[C]]. Accuracy is computed on ground-truth-clear pairs as the fraction of
verdicts that select the objectively correct side. Position bias contrasts
the rate at which the first-presented answer wins among non-tie verdicts
against the 0.5 null; the flip rate is the fraction of pairs whose
canonically-mapped verdict differs between the two presentation orders.
Length bias is the rate at which the longer answer wins among non-tie
verdicts minus 0.5. Family preference is measured on kin pairs, where
exactly one side comes from the judge's own family: the headline statistic
is the kin-wrong pick rate, the fraction of ground-truth-clear kin pairs in
which the judge selects its own family's answer although the reference
answer proves it wrong. Inter-judge agreement uses pairwise Cohen's kappa on
the three-class verdicts of canonical-order judgements
\citep{cohen1960coefficient}. A pooled logistic regression with judge fixed
effects estimates the simultaneous effects of length difference, kinship,
and an answer-quality difference indicator (derived from the correctness
labels, so that both-correct and both-wrong pairs also inform the length and
kinship coefficients) on the probability that Assistant A wins, providing a
multivariate check on the univariate rates.

\subsection{Zero-Cost Constraint}
\label{sec:free}

The entire study runs on free API quotas with hard spending protection
enabled: every open-weight model is capped at its complimentary
one-million-token allocation, after which the provider rejects further calls
instead of billing them, and the two proprietary judges run on their
provider's zero-priced promotional flash tier under platform rate limits.
Token consumption was logged per model throughout, and the
total footprint of the experiment---8.4 million tokens across roughly
11,700 API calls on the two original platforms, plus 1.0 million tokens
across 824 calls on the third platform at revision time---stayed within
the complimentary budget. This
constraint is not merely logistical; it demonstrates that a full
multi-family judge audit is reproducible by any research group at zero
monetary cost, which we view as part of the contribution.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Two free-quota exhaustions shape the coverage picture and are reported
transparently. GLM-5.3 exhausted its complimentary allocation after 714 of
800 main-pass judgements (89\%; its coverage is complete for mathematics and
nearly complete for knowledge, the two verifiable domains), and
Qwen3.8-2.4T completed 782 of 800 (98\%); both models could not participate
in the test-retest subset, which therefore covers eight open judges. All
remaining judges have complete coverage. Agreement statistics use
pairwise-available pairs, so partial coverage degrades precision rather than
introducing selection bias into the headline metrics. In total the study
collected 9,453 usable main-pass verdicts and 640 test-retest verdicts.
The proprietary judges completed the main pass in full, but their retest
pass was curtailed by platform rate limits and is excluded from headline
test-retest statistics; the raw partial records are released with the
artifact for transparency.

\subsection{Compliance and Decision Severity}
\label{sec:res-compliance}

Instruction compliance is nearly universal: eleven of twelve judges parse
successfully on at least 99\% of calls, and the only notable exception is
GLM-5.3 at 94.4\%, whose mandatory reasoning phase occasionally consumes the
reply budget before the verdict token is emitted; the one-reminder parse
retry is invoked on only 1.2\% of calls overall, rising to 9.4\% for
GLM-5.3. Compliance, however, is
the weakest notion of reliability. Decision severity---how often a judge
commits to a winner at all---separates the panel far more sharply, and it
separates by family. The five Qwen judges tie on 15--31\% of canonical-order
pairs, whereas DeepSeek-V4-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Pro tie on 50\% and 59\%
respectively, GLM-5.3 on 47\%, and Kimi-K3 on 44\%. The two proprietary
references bracket the open panel---Agnes-3.0-Flash ties on 28\% of pairs,
Agnes-2.5-Flash on 44\%---so severity collapse is not an artifact of
open-source models or of free tiers specifically; it is a
serving-and-tuning choice that happens to differ systematically across
providers. Because ties are excluded from conditional bias statistics and
scored as errors in accuracy, this single parameter reshapes every downstream
metric for the affected judges, as the following subsections show.

\subsection{Ground-Truth Accuracy}
\label{sec:res-acc}

Figure~\ref{fig:accuracy} reports accuracy on the 66 ground-truth-clear
pairs. The Qwen family forms the top cluster (0.64--0.71), with the two
flash checkpoints ahead of the larger tiers; GLM-5.3 reaches 0.61 and
Kimi-K3 0.50. The DeepSeek family splits: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash achieves a
usable 0.56, but DeepSeek-V4-Flash (0.42) and DeepSeek-V4-Pro (0.32) fall
below the 0.5 chance line. DeepSeek-V4-Pro's below-chance score is a direct
consequence of severity collapse: on 59\% of pairs it abstains with a tie,
which the strict scoring counts as an error. Its 0.32 is thus best read as
``right one time in three, silent most of the rest'' rather than as active
misjudgement, and the same reading applies with less force to
DeepSeek-V4-Flash. Within-family scale gradients are accordingly
non-monotonic for DeepSeek (the flagship Pro tier is the \emph{worst} family
member) and roughly flat for Qwen, so capability tier alone is a poor
predictor of judging usefulness---a finding we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:res-scale}.

\subsection{Position Bias and Order Consistency}
\label{sec:res-pb}

Four open judges show a statistically significant first-position preference
(bootstrap CI excluding 0.5): Qwen3.8-27B at $+0.113$ and
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash at $+0.146$ are the strongest, with the two flash Qwens
just past the significance line ($+0.065$ to $+0.075$). The remaining
judges sit near zero, including the two largest Qwen tiers
($-0.013$ and $-0.028$). Average position bias is therefore mild by the
standards of early GPT-4-class reports, but the order-swap flip rates
expose substantial hidden instability: between 12.5\% and 32\% of pairs
receive different canonical verdicts when the presentation order is
swapped. Two judges served with mandatory reasoning are the most
order-stable (Qwen3.8-2.4T at 0.125, GLM-5.3 at 0.138), and within the
Qwen family the flip rate falls with tier (0.32 and 0.26 for the two
flash generations, 0.20--0.31 for the mid and max tiers, 0.13 for the
2.4T flagship). Scale and
thinking mode thus buy consistency rather than calibration.

\subsection{Length Bias and the Anti-Verbosity Instruction}
\label{sec:res-lb}

The judging rubric explicitly instructs models not to reward length, and
in the naive pooled model of the main pass eight of ten open judges tilt
against the longer answer (length bias $-0.03$ to $-0.12$;
DeepSeek-V4-Pro at $+0.112$ is the only meaningfully length-favouring
judge, and the proprietary references show the same signature, one of them
in amplified form). That tilt is fragile, however: under question-level
clustering the pooled coefficient is $-0.46$ per 1{,}000 characters of
full length difference ($p=0.022$) but $-0.63$ and non-significant
($p=0.24$) for the length difference the judge actually sees, and none of
the thirty per-judge length coefficients survives Holm correction. The
robustness experiments (Section~\ref{sec:robust}) settle the causal
question directly: across a four-arm prompt ablation---original rubric,
rubric with the anti-verbosity sentence deleted, a softened variant, and a
length-normalised variant---the longer-answer pick rate stays between
0.476 and 0.490 and the original-versus-no-sentence interaction is noise
($+0.05$, one-sided $p=0.93$). The anti-verbosity sentence neither causes
nor corrects length behaviour on this benchmark; the apparent inversion of
the verbosity bias should be read as a property of this roster and
stimulus set, not of the instruction, and length behaviour must be probed
directly with length-stratified pairs rather than managed by prompt
editing.

\subsection{Family Preference under Ground-Truth Control}
\label{sec:res-kin}

Figure~\ref{fig:kin} and Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarise kinship
behaviour, and the headline is heterogeneity: kinship effects are
family-specific rather than universal. Pooling judges within family, Qwen
judges pick a same-family answer in only 37\% of non-tie kin pairs (CI
$[0.31, 0.43]$, $n=950$)---a significant kin-\emph{avoidance} (regression
$-0.64$, $p=2\times10^{-4}$)---while GLM-5.3 rewards its own family's
answers ($+1.10$, $p=1\times10^{-4}$); DeepSeek and Kimi show no
family-level effect in the main pass (Fisher $p=0.11$ and $0.16$). The
quality-controlled statistic sharpens the contrast for the large cells and
stays directional for the small ones: when the reference answer proves the
kin side wrong, Qwen judges still choose it in only 5.2\% of cases
($n=96$), GLM-5.3 in 27.3\% ($n=11$), and Kimi-K3 in 33.3\% ($n=9$); the
GLM and Kimi kin-wrong samples are small, so we read them as directional
evidence rather than precise estimates. GLM-5.3 is the sharpest
illustration of preference overriding evidence: on the ground-truth-clear
kin pairs where its own family is right it picks kin on every one, yet on
those where its family is wrong it still picks kin more than one time in
four, and the same pattern holds in the mathematics domain alone (kin-win
0.857, $n=14$), which is free of the labeller circularity that affects the
knowledge domain. These estimates pool family judges rather than
individuals, and the pooled sign is a property of this roster's family mix,
not a law of self-preference. The robustness experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}) test whether these signals replicate on new,
harder verifiable tasks.

\subsection{Agreement Structure}
\label{sec:res-agree}

Pairwise Cohen's kappa on canonical-order verdicts spans 0.27--0.72 with a
median of 0.54 (Figure~\ref{fig:agreement})---moderate agreement, broadly
in line with published audits of commercial judges. Three structural
patterns stand out. First, the highest-agreement pairs sit within the Qwen
family: the two Qwen3.8 flash generations agree at $\kappa=0.72$, and the
max and 2.4T tiers at $0.70$; the strongest cross-family pairs reach the
same level (Qwen3.8-2.4T with GLM-5.3 at $0.70$, GLM-5.3 with Kimi-K3 at
$0.70$). The open judges agree with the proprietary references (mean
$\kappa=0.53$) slightly less than they agree with each other ($0.54$), so
proprietary status is neither a guarantee of a distinct viewpoint nor a
privileged one. Second, the family-blocked means reveal a cross-family
fault line between Qwen and DeepSeek ($\kappa=0.44$, the lowest open--open
block) while GLM pairs strongly with Kimi ($0.70$) and with
Agnes-2.5-Flash ($0.64$). Third, the judges whose severity collapsed are
also the least agreeable ones: DeepSeek-V4-Pro's mean kappa of 0.43 and
the minimum pair Qwen3.7-Flash versus DeepSeek-V4-Pro at 0.27 show that
abstention behaviour, not disagreement about winners, is what most damages
raw agreement statistics.

\subsection{Test--Retest Stability}
\label{sec:res-retest}

Repeating a 40-pair subset in canonical order at temperature zero yields
agreement between repeats of 0.75--0.97 across the eight open judges with
remaining quota; with $n=40$ per judge these estimates carry wide
intervals. DeepSeek-V4-Pro is the least self-consistent (0.75) and
Qwen3.8-27B the most (0.97). For DeepSeek-V4-Pro the raw 0.75 mostly
reflects the reproducibility of its abstentions: it is stable in returning
ties, which inflates raw consistency while contributing nothing to
discrimination, and its order-stability and decision quality are
correspondingly weak. This decoupling of consistency from validity echoes
recent multi-judge audits and cautions against using test--retest
agreement alone as a certification of judge quality. The robustness
experiments extend the measurement to a cross-session setting: a 60-pair
retest conducted weeks after the main pass (Section~\ref{sec:robust})
agrees with the original canonical verdicts at 0.85--0.92, comparable to
the intra-session values, so temperature-zero stability persists across
sessions for the judges with remaining quota.

\subsection{Panel Behaviour: Accuracy Gains without Debiasing}
\label{sec:res-panel}

Figure~\ref{fig:panel} evaluates majority-vote panels of $k$ judges by
exact enumeration over all $\binom{10}{3}=120$ and $\binom{10}{5}=252$
combinations, scored under the unified convention (ties count as errors).
A panel of three lifts ground-truth accuracy from a single-judge mean of
0.582 (best single 0.712) to 0.696 (CI $[0.533, 0.845]$)---level with,
not above, the best single judge---and five judges add a further $+0.029$
to 0.725; panels drawn from all twelve judges including the proprietary
references reach 0.693/0.721. The larger effect is on decisiveness:
abstention falls from 30\% (the single-judge mean tie rate on
ground-truth-clear pairs) to 17.8\% ($k=3$) and 14.3\% ($k=5$), while
decision quality on the pairs where the panel commits stays flat (0.834
single versus 0.846--0.847 panel). Panels are thus decisiveness
instruments rather than accuracy miracles. Position bias, however, is
neither amplified nor cancelled: on the same ground-truth-clear pair set,
mean signed first-position preference is $+0.037$ for single judges,
$+0.036$ for panels of three, and $+0.040$ for panels of five.
Order-swap aggregation on top of the panel adds only $+0.011$ accuracy
(0.707 versus 0.696) at higher abstention (0.194), so order swapping
remains mandatory at the single-judge level while its marginal benefit
above a panel is modest; panels should combine judges from different
families and rely on
aggregation.

\subsection{Scale Trends within Families}
\label{sec:res-scale}

Figure~\ref{fig:scale} plots accuracy, absolute position bias, and flip
rate against within-family tier. The Qwen family shows a clean and, to our
knowledge, previously unreported gradient in \emph{consistency}: flip rate
falls from 0.26--0.32 across the flash and mid tiers to 0.13 for the 2.4T
flagship, while accuracy stays within a narrow 0.64--0.71 band. The DeepSeek family inverts the intuitive ordering: the
Pro flagship is the least accurate and least agreeable family member
because of severity collapse. Family identity and tuning choices dominate
raw parameter count as determinants of judge behaviour, which is why the
practical guidance of the next section keys on screening rather than on
scale.

\section{Robustness Experiments}
\label{sec:robust}

The revision adds a pre-registered confirmatory battery (analysis plan
released with the artifact) that re-examines the headline claims under the
same zero-cost constraint. All experiments reuse the main-pass prompt and
truncation settings, and every call logs the model code, the
platform-reported snapshot id, and the date. A snapshot-stability check
first re-judged 20 stratified pairs from the main pass in both orders on
two judges weeks after the original run: canonical-verdict agreement was
0.875 (Kimi-K3) and 0.925 (Qwen3.8-27B), both above the pre-registered
0.85 threshold, so main-pass and new data are pooled and the main-pass
prompt arm serves as the ablation baseline.

\paragraph{Instruction ablation (E1).}
Four prompt arms---the original rubric (P1), the rubric with the
anti-verbosity sentence deleted (P0), a softened variant (P2), and a
length-normalised variant (P3)---were run on 60 length-stratified pairs
covering all four domains with four judges from three families (720 new
verdicts; the P1 arm reuses the main-pass canonical-order verdicts). The
result is a clean negative: longer-answer pick rates are 0.481 (P1),
0.490 (P0), 0.476 (P2), and 0.480 (P3); the P1-versus-P0 length
interaction is $+0.05$ (one-sided $p=0.93$); and per-arm length
coefficients span $-0.03$ to $+0.09$. The anti-verbosity sentence neither
causes nor corrects length behaviour on this benchmark, and the main
pass's anti-length tilt is accordingly reclassified as a fragile
descriptive pattern (pooled clustering $p=0.022$ full-length, $p=0.24$
judge-visible; no per-judge coefficient survives Holm).

\paragraph{Tie ablation (E2).}
Severity collapse was re-tested on 44 tie-prone pairs (selected before
judging; by construction they contain no ground-truth-clear pair, which
itself shows that ties concentrate on genuinely close answers) with four
judges and two prompt revisions. Forcing a choice (T1: ``a tie is not an
available verdict'') eliminates ties entirely for every judge (0.00
versus 0.36--0.86 under the original rubric; exact McNemar, Holm
$p\le 0.0002$). Tightening the definition of a tie (T2: ties only for
substantive equivalence) removes the DeepSeek excess (0.50--0.61 versus
0.82--0.86, Holm $p\le 0.003$) but leaves the Qwen judges unchanged
(0.23--0.32, Holm $p\ge 0.22$). Severity collapse is therefore largely
prompt-contingent, and the practical fix is a one-line prompt change
rather than a different model.

\paragraph{Harder verifiable tasks and kin replication (E3/E4).}
To test the kinship signals on harder, fully verifiable material, 140 new
tasks were built from competition mathematics (MATH Levels 4--5 with
numeric answers), unit-tested code completion (HumanEval), and
hard-subject multiple choice (MMLU); correctness is decided by frozen
deterministic parsers and executed unit tests, with no LLM labeller
anywhere in the loop. Four generators answered all tasks (560 verified
answers; in the second batch a second Qwen generator model code with its
own free allocation replaced a quota-exhausted one, logged per call), and
93 ground-truth-clear pairs (50 Qwen-wrong, 31 DeepSeek-wrong, 12
Kimi-wrong) were judged by four judges spanning the three families with
remaining quota. The main-pass family-level signals do not replicate at
this difficulty: kin-side error rates are 9.1\\% for Qwen judges versus a
16.7\\% non-kin baseline (Fisher $p=0.18$) and 0\\% for DeepSeek versus
7.7\\% ($p=0.25$); the self/brother decomposition shows no
self-favouritism either (Qwen self 6.7\\% versus brother 10.1\\%). The
single exception is Kimi, whose judges err on 4 of 12 kin-wrong pairs
(33\\%) against a 2.3\\% baseline (Fisher $p=0.006$)---a small-sample
signal we report as exploratory. The honest summary: family-level kin
favouritism is weaker and less general than the main pass suggested; the
main-pass evidence for GLM ($+1.10$) and Qwen ($-0.64$) stands as
measured, but it does not transfer to harder verifiable tasks for the
families we could re-test.

\paragraph{Deployment without references (E5).}
Removing the reference answer from the judging prompt changes nothing
measurable for the judges with with-reference twins (27 and 24 pairs
respectively): paired accuracy differences are 0.00 for both (95\\%
cluster-bootstrap CIs $[-0.19, 0.19]$ and $[-0.13, 0.13]$), with tie
rates moving by at most $+7$ points. A third judge without a
with-reference twin shows 77.5\\% no-reference accuracy, consistent with
the paired estimates. Free judges do not lean on the reference block for
these verifiable domains.

\paragraph{Cross-session stability (E8).}
A 60-pair retest weeks after the main pass, on three judges with
remaining quota, agrees with the original canonical verdicts at 0.85
(DeepSeek-V4-Flash), 0.87 (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash), and 0.92
(Qwen3.8-27B)---level with the intra-session retest values despite the
time gap.

\paragraph{Third-platform deployment check (E7a).}
A third free platform (the Intern AI Discovery API) serves four of our
judges under the same model codes. We re-ran each on 47--48 stratified
main-pass cells with the identical prompt at temperature zero; repeated
identical calls confirmed determinism first (13 of 15 probe cells
verdict-stable; the two exceptions were a truncation artifact and one
borderline pair), so residual disagreement reflects deployment rather
than sampling. Same-code raw agreement with the original platform was
0.875 ($\kappa=0.811$) for GLM-5.3, 0.723 ($\kappa=0.565$) for
Qwen3.8-27B, 0.708 ($\kappa=0.565$) for DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and 0.583
($\kappa=0.379$) for DeepSeek-V4-Flash, with per-model tie rates close
to the originals throughout. A free-tier model code is therefore not a
behavioural constant: one of four judges replicated at self-consistency
level, two replicated partially, and one diverged materially, so
cross-platform reuse of published judge verdicts requires an explicit
same-code check.

\paragraph{GLM-5.3 coverage restoration (E7b).}
GLM-5.3 passes a pre-registered compatibility gate (42/48 raw agreement,
threshold 0.85), so we used the third platform to restore its 126-cell
coverage gap from the main pass: 115 cells were recovered before the
platform's daily complimentary token quota ($\sim$1M tokens) was
exhausted, raising GLM coverage from 674/800 to 789/800 cells (98.6\%).
The gap decomposes into 40 unparseable outputs and 86 never-completed
calls, and it was strongly domain-skewed (62 of the 86 unwritten cells
fell in the summarisation domain), so the seeded-shuffle MCAR claim
needs refinement: missingness was uniform over pairs and orders but not
over domains. The restoration measures the consequence directly: on the
recovered cells GLM ties at 0.296 versus 0.491 on the originally
available subset ($p<10^{-3}$), revising its all-cells tie rate from
0.491 to 0.463 on the merged view. Every other headline moves inside its
interval: GT-clear accuracy stays at 0.606, the order-swap flip rate
moves from 0.138 to 0.158, and mean kappa against the other judges from
0.604 to 0.590, so the frozen v1 values in Table~\ref{tab:summary}
remain conservative and the revision direction is now measured rather
than assumed.

\paragraph{Cross-platform retest for GLM-5.3 (E7c).}
GLM-5.3 was the one judge whose quota died before the original retest
battery. It now completes a retest on the third platform over the same
40 seeded pairs: on the 20 pairs with both repeats parsed, repeat-to-
repeat and repeat-to-main agreement are both 20/20, sitting at the top of
the original judges' 0.75--0.97 retest band despite crossing platforms
(two of the 20 main cells come from the E7b restoration).

\paragraph{Premium reference anchors (E7d).}
The third platform also serves two latest-generation models from
families outside the entire generator and judge roster: Intern-S2
(Shanghai AI Laboratory) and MiniMax-M3. We adopt both as external
premium anchors under the identical protocol. The platform's daily quota admitted a
194-cell pilot (12\% of the planned $2\times800$ grid). Intern-S2 shows
100\% verdict compliance, a 0.484 tie rate, GT-clear accuracy 0.636
($n=11$), zero order-swap flips on its 6 double-covered pairs, a
longer-answer pick rate of 0.191 that matches the anti-length signature
already documented for the free tier, and kappa 0.47--0.84 (median 0.71)
against the twelve judges, aligning closest with the top free-tier
judges (0.836 with Qwen3.8-2.4T, 0.809 with Qwen3.8-Flash). MiniMax-M3
shows kappa 0.37--0.69 (median 0.54) with its highest alignment to
GLM-5.3 (0.691) and Agnes-2.5 (0.653). Pilot $n$'s are reported as
such; the remaining grid ($\sim$1.2M tokens) exceeds a day of
complimentary quota, so the resumable runner and its state ship with the
artifact and complete automatically across quota windows.

\paragraph{Not run.}
The thinking-mode contrast (E9) could not be run: both forced-thinking
models exhausted their free quota. The premium-anchor grid (E7d) is
complete as a 194-cell pilot; the remaining anchor cells, 11 GLM
restoration cells, and one spot-check cell stay queued in the released
runner and require no human intervention. A de-identified
human-annotation kit (110 pairs plus 10 calibration pairs, three-way
protocol, three annotators) is released with the artifact; its labels
were still pending at revision time, so all reliability claims in this
paper remain at the judge--judge level.

The audit yields one message above all: \emph{free judges are usable, but
only after screening, and never naively}. The properties that matter most
are not the ones model cards advertise. Parameter count and flagship status
predict neither accuracy nor severity; the DeepSeek Pro flagship is the
least useful judge in the roster precisely because it abstains on most
pairs, while a small flash checkpoint from the same provider discriminates
better. What predicts behaviour is a combination of prompt contingency
(severity), roster--stimulus properties (length), lineage (kinship,
weakly), and deployment configuration (thinking mode for order
stability). We distill the findings into six guidelines for zero-cost
judging. We distill
the findings into six guidelines for zero-cost judging.

\paragraph{G1: Screen for severity collapse first---and fix it with the
prompt.}
Before deploying any free judge, run a twenty-pair probe with a known
quality difference and measure the tie rate. On ground-truth-clear pairs
the main-pass tie rates are 47\% (DeepSeek-V4-Flash) and 59\%
(DeepSeek-V4-Pro), with GLM-5.3 (32\%) and Kimi-K3 (38\%) near the line
as threshold observations. A judge that abstains on clearly-different
pairs will undermine any downstream use that assumes
discrimination---leaderboards, win-rate estimation, best-of-$n$
selection---regardless of how sound its individual verdicts are. The tie
ablation shows that the cheapest remedy is a prompt revision, not a
different model: forcing a choice eliminates ties entirely (Holm
$p\le 0.0002$), and tightening the tie definition removes the DeepSeek
excess specifically.

\paragraph{G2: Prefer Qwen Max and, where quota allows, the 2.4T
flagship as workhorses.}
Within this roster, Qwen3.8-Max and Qwen3.8-2.4T offer the best
combination of hard-flip stability (0.117 and 0.060; hard flips change
the canonical verdict, soft ones only convert ties to decisions) and
clean position behaviour. Two caveats: 2.4T is served with mandatory
reasoning, emits at the output cap on 17.8\% of calls, and consumed
roughly double the tokens of the flash tiers, and both models' free-quota
headroom is typically the scarcest in the Qwen family. Qwen3.8-27B,
recommended in the initial pass, is demoted: under the two-way convention
its first-position bias survives Holm correction at $+0.139$, the
largest in the roster, so we no longer call it stable.

\paragraph{G3: Order swapping is mandatory for single judges; above a
panel its extra benefit is modest.}
Even the most order-stable judge flips on one pair in eight, and the
least stable on one in three, so single-judge deployments must judge in
both orders and aggregate on the canonical verdict. Above a majority
panel, however, order-swap aggregation adds only $+0.011$ accuracy (0.707
versus 0.696) while raising abstention (0.178 to 0.194), so panels should
combine judges from different families and balance positions across the
pool rather than pay for per-pair double-judging of everything.

\paragraph{G4: Do not manage length behaviour by prompt editing.}
The initial pass attributed an anti-length tilt to the anti-verbosity
sentence; the four-arm ablation refutes the attribution: arm differences
are within noise (interaction $p=0.93$) and no per-judge length
coefficient survives Holm correction. Prompt edits are the wrong lever
for length behaviour on this benchmark. If length matters to your use
case, probe it directly with length-stratified pairs and, where possible,
standardise candidate lengths before comparison; re-probe after any
roster or task change, because the tilt appears to be a property of the
roster--stimulus combination.

\paragraph{G5: Audit kinship with ground truth before trusting a judge
on its own ecosystem.}
Family preference is real but lineage-specific and, on harder verifiable
tasks, weak: the main pass found Qwen avoidance ($-0.64$) and GLM reward
($+1.10$), while the replication on new tasks found no family-level
effect for the re-testable families. Any evaluation that uses a judge
from family $X$ to rank models that include family $X$ should run a
ground-truth-controlled kin probe first. Size the probe for power:
detecting a 30\%-versus-8\% kin-side error difference at 80\% power needs
about 50 kin-wrong pairs per family---roughly 0.1M free-tier tokens---not
a token-sized spot check; our own Kimi cell ($n=12$) illustrates how
small cells produce exploratory-only results.

\paragraph{G6: Build panels across families, not across tiers.}
The accuracy case for cross-family panels is weaker than the initial pass
suggested---same-family, two-family, and three-family panels of three
score 0.713, 0.697, and 0.688 with overlapping intervals and the ordering
reversed---so we rest the recommendation on the agreement structure
instead: family-level fault lines (Qwen--DeepSeek mean $\kappa=0.44$)
mean that a three-judge panel drawn from three families decorrelates
errors far more effectively than three tiers of the same family, which
matters because the shared position component passes through majority
voting unchanged while the panel converts abstentions into decisions at
zero monetary cost.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
Our findings refine three strands of the literature. The verbosity-bias
literature documents that judges favour longer answers; our four-arm
ablation shows the standard mitigation sentence is behaviourally inert on
this benchmark---neither causing nor correcting the tilt---and that the
apparent inversion does not survive clustered inference, a caution for
length-controlled win-rate claims that do not account for question-level
clustering. The self-preference literature establishes that judges recognise
and reward their own generations; we generalise the unit of analysis from model to family, show that the
effect's \emph{direction} varies by lineage, and add a replication on
harder verifiable tasks in which the family-level effect does not
transfer, which matters for the increasingly common situation where one
provider both ships the judge and supplies some of the candidates. Finally,
panel proposals argue that judge diversity averages away idiosyncratic
errors; our panel analysis refines this: diversity cancels the independent
noise component strongly enough to lift accuracy markedly, but the shared
position component passes through majority voting unchanged, so panels must
still pair diversity with order-balanced aggregation.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Our findings should be read against several limitations. First, the ten
open-source judges are all served through a single provider endpoint, and the
two proprietary judges through a second one. Although this removes serving
differences as a confound within each group, it also means that our
observations reflect the providers' quantization and serving configurations
of each checkpoint rather than the raw open weights, and replication on
self-hosted inference may differ in detail; in particular, the Agnes platform
injects undisclosed system instructions ahead of our judging prompt, so the
proprietary judges' absolute numbers are indicative rather than strictly
comparable to the open panel. The revision adds a direct measurement of
how much deployment identity matters: same-code re-runs on a third
platform agree at only 0.58--0.88 raw depending on the model
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}), so serving configuration is a first-order
source of variation in its own right. Second, the roster covers four
model families that all originate from Chinese development teams, because
these are the families with usable free tiers on the chosen platform at the
time of the study; Western open families such as Llama or Mistral were not
available under the zero-cost constraint, so our family-preference findings
should be generalized to those families with care, and the GLM family is
represented by a single checkpoint, so GLM-specific conclusions rest on one
observation. Third, two judges are
served with a mandatory reasoning phase, which is entangled with their
family identity and tier; we treat thinking mode as an observational
variable and report where it plausibly matters, but a fully crossed
thinking-by-family design is not realizable on this platform. Fourth, the
evaluation uses 400 pairs over four English-language domains. While the
stratified design gives every judge the same stimulus set and the bootstrap
accounts for question-level clustering, the absolute number of
ground-truth-clear kin pairs per family remains modest, and kin-preference
estimates for individual judges carry correspondingly wide intervals. Fifth,
our generators produce single answers per question at a moderate sampling
temperature; verdicts could vary with additional samples, and we leave
multi-sample generation to future work. Finally, the two open-ended domains
lack ground truth, so for those domains our reliability claims are about agreement among
judges rather than closeness to correctness. The confirmatory battery has
coverage limits that we report rather than paper over: the thinking-mode contrast (E9) could not be run under the zero-cost
constraint and the premium-anchor grid (E7d) is complete only as a
194-cell pilot within the third platform's daily complimentary quota, the kin replication cells for DeepSeek ($n=31$)
and Kimi ($n=12$) are small and exploratory, GLM-5.3's original coverage gap was restored to 98.6\% on the third
platform with the domain-skew correction quantified in
Section~\ref{sec:robust}, and the
human-annotation kit released with the artifact was still unlabeled at
revision time, so all reliability claims remain at the judge--judge
level.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We audited the judging behaviour of every LLM reachable at zero
monetary cost on three public platforms at the time of study: ten
open-weight free-tier judges from four families and two proprietary
free-tier references, extended at revision with same-code replication,
coverage restoration, and two premium anchors through a third free
platform, evaluated under one protocol over 400 answer pairs
in four domains, with both presentation orders, a test--retest subset,
and ground-truth-controlled family-preference probes, followed by a
pre-registered confirmatory battery (prompt ablations, harder verifiable
tasks, reference-free deployment, and cross-session retests). The picture
that emerges is nuanced but actionable. Free judges are viable: the
strongest open checkpoints reach 0.71 accuracy on verifiable pairs and
inter-judge agreement comparable to published commercial audits. But
viability is conditional on screening, because the failure modes are
systematic rather than random: severity collapse that is prompt-contingent
and fixable with a one-line prompt change, an apparent anti-length tilt
that proves fragile under clustered inference and inert to prompt
revision, lineage-specific kin favouritism in the main pass that does not
transfer to harder verifiable tasks, and majority voting that inherits,
rather than removes, the shared position bias while converting
abstentions into decisions. Every one of these failure modes is
detectable with probes that cost a few thousand free-tier tokens, and
every probe ships with the released artifact, so the audit and its
screening kit can be re-run by any group as free-tier catalogues evolve.
As evaluation infrastructure comes to rest on machine judgement, the
reliability of the judges themselves---especially the ones that cost
nothing---deserves the same systematic attention that has been lavished
on the systems they grade.
